Drift Scorecard

A free Mac tool that compares AI video clips with a reference picture and marks each one PASS, UNSURE or FAIL.

Public on GitHub

The problem

AI video can look right in the first frame, then slowly move away from the look of the series. I wanted a way to measure that automatically.

What I built

Drift Scorecard measures saturation, brightness, background and overall look against a reference picture. It checks a whole folder at once: 31 clips took about 4 seconds on my Mac. Clips just outside a limit are marked UNSURE, so a person decides.

  • A one-file report that works offline. Drag a limit slider and every result, graph and count updates at once.
  • Quick review. Hover over a clip to play through its frames, or drag across it to compare it with the reference.
  • Checks itself against you. Press Keep or Redo on clips and see how well the current limits agree with your decisions.
  • A Mac app. Drag in a picture and a folder, and watch a progress bar. The report also has filters, search, keyboard shortcuts, CSV export, and light and dark themes.

Built since, not released yet: because of what the retest showed, the next version also checks every clip against the look of the whole series, checks start pictures before anything is rendered, lists sharp jumps between neighbouring clips, and can have AI check frames against a written list of series rules. These are on my Mac and haven’t been tested on a real episode.

What broke

  • In the first version, the default limits rejected two clips I had approved.
  • Most clips were redos, so marking every clip for a redo would score 27 of 31. A raw accuracy number would have flattered the tool.
  • My first test couldn’t separate drift from a change of scene, because every clip was compared with one shared picture. That made its scores hard to read.
  • File handling can look like drift. During development, a test video with no color label measured 83% saturation against its own source picture.

What changed

  • I tried lowering the background limit to 60%. That accepted every approved clip, but also let five redo clips through.
  • Those five were rejected mostly for problems this version can’t see, such as objects or characters changing shape or appearance. That shows what to build next.
  • I added the UNSURE result, which cut clear mistakes on the standard limits from 3 to 2 by sending borderline clips to a person.
  • Checking five frames instead of three, and adding overall look at its standard limit, changed no results on this episode.
  • I retested every clip against its own start picture. No approved clip failed, but 17 of the 27 redo clips passed, so I now describe it as a measurement, not a judge of quality.
  • With the correct color label, the test video measured 99.7%, so I fixed the test video rather than loosening the limits.

Real results

Read this first: the first table doesn’t tell the whole story. Every clip was compared with one shared picture, so a clip from a different scene could fail just for being a different scene. The retest further down shows what changes when each clip gets its own start picture, and neither test is enough alone.

I ran it on all 31 clips from one episode of a real AI-generated video series, scored against the same reference picture. Then I compared each result with my own production decision: 4 clips were approved and 27 were marked for a redo.

Retest with each clip’s own start picture

I retested the same 31 clips against each clip’s own start picture, in a version that isn’t released yet. The opening frames now closely matched their pictures, which confirms the pairing.

LimitsApproved clips (4)Redo clips (27)Clearly rightClearly wrongLeft to a person
Standard3 passed, 1 unsure17 passed, 10 failed13 of 3117 of 311 of 31
Cartoon preset4 passed17 passed, 10 failed14 of 3117 of 310 of 31

No approved clip failed, which is the improvement I wanted. But 17 of the 27 redo clips passed. My production notes show why: all 17 had problems this tool can’t measure, including character look (16), objects (11), and body or movement (10). And 15 of the 17 had also drifted in color, brightness or background from the series look. A clip’s own start picture can’t catch that, because a faded start picture produces a faded clip that matches it.

So the two tests catch different things. One shared picture catches drift from the series look, but can’t tell it from a change of scene. A clip’s own start picture removes the scene problem, but only measures change within the clip. My notes list every problem on a clip, not which one decided the redo. The limits also haven’t been re-tuned for this method. It measures color, brightness, background and general resemblance against a start picture. It doesn’t judge whether a clip is good.

Limits of this test: this is one episode from one creator, with only four approved clips, so none of these numbers should be read as general accuracy. Some redo decisions used earlier color and background checks that work much like this tool, so the comparison isn’t fully independent. The approved clips were also re-rendered later than the others, which may affect their scores. The tool measures color, background and general resemblance only, so a person still needs to review each clip.
